      Lisa Cirenza

      Lisa Cirenza is a painter in the French Alps. Shaped by her time as a young researcher on the Juneau Icefield, she paints light, water, rock and time (ice, snow, fog, lake and mountains) in encaustic, mixed media, oil and watercolour.

      01Artist statement

      Statement

      Most people look up and see the Alps. I see a record of time being erased.

      For as long as I've painted, my work has been about connection: to each other, to the natural world, and to what lies beneath the surface of what we think we see. For years I painted people: strangers sharing a carriage on the London Underground, refugees, the lives carried inside a face, and the forces that divide us. We have never been so connected on the surface, nor so disconnected underneath. My current work follows those forces into the natural world and turns to light, water, rock and time, the things that change too slowly or too quickly for a glance, and can only be seen by someone who stops to look.

      Long before I painted it, I was a field researcher on glaciological expeditions across the Juneau Icefield, part of an international team of scientists measuring a landscape in motion. On the ice, as beautiful as it was hostile, where I once came close to dying of hypothermia, I learned that watching closely is a matter of survival, and an act of hope. I photographed it all, promising myself I would paint it one day. Decades later, much of what those photographs hold has melted away. Painting them now is a conversation between then and now.

      My new paintings ask for the same thing the ice asked of me: a few minutes of real attention, in a world that rarely asks for more than a second.

      02Long bio

      Layers of a life

      1. 01

        Beneath the surface

        Lisa Cirenza is a painter based in the French Alps whose work explores connection, and what lies beneath the surface of what we think we see.

      2. 02

        Seventeen, on the Juneau Icefield

        At seventeen and eighteen, as a National Science Foundation scholar with the Juneau Icefield Research Program, she worked as a field researcher on glaciological expeditions across the Juneau Icefield in Alaska and British Columbia, among an international team of scientists. She led trail parties from camp to camp, dug twenty-foot snow pits and sketched their layers of ice and snow to read the glacier's history, took core samples, measured the movement of glaciers and icefalls, and studied the fragile mosses and fungi that survive on the ice. She photographed it all, promising herself she would paint it one day. She later co-authored The Power of Ice (Chicago Children's Press, under her maiden name, Lisa Gitkin), a book illustrated with her expedition photographs that introduces children aged nine to twelve to glaciers and to life as a research scientist on the ice.

      3. 03

        Stanford, Paris, and the world of business

        She went on to study applied earth sciences at Stanford University, including studies at the École des Mines de Paris, and completed a master's in international policy studies there. Told by her parents that she would never survive as an artist, she set out to learn business from the best, in oil and gas corporate finance at Goldman Sachs and as a consultant at Booz Allen Hamilton, before returning to painting on her own terms.

      4. 04

        From people to ice

        Trained in old-masters oil technique, she later studied sumi ink under a master in Japan, and has worked across oil, digital media, watercolour and encaustic. For years she painted people: strangers sharing a carriage on the London Underground, refugees she worked with in Milan, the lives carried inside a face, and the political forces pulling societies apart. Her work has long centred on human connection in a world that is connected on the surface and increasingly disconnected underneath. That early work left little empty space, because her life then had little: a nomadic life across three continents, in several languages and cultures, at the pace of big cities. Moving to the French Alps taught her to breathe, and brought her back to reading the landscape for its history, and to the heartbreak of watching the local glaciers disappear as the climate changes. Trained in both earth sciences and international policy, she has always watched how systems under stress break, and what lets them recompose: first human ones, now the ice. Her current work turns to light, water, rock and time, and to the icefield photographs she took decades ago, of a landscape that has since largely melted away. It is work about loss, but also about attention and hope: what we truly see, we are more likely to protect.

      5. 05

        Collections and exhibitions

        Her work is held in the Stanford Hospital collection alongside works by David Hockney and Robert Rauschenberg, and in private collections in Europe, the United States and Asia. It has been praised in the Financial Times and exhibited at the Grand Palais in Paris, in Venice during the Biennale, at the Asian Art Biennale in Dhaka, and in New York, Miami, London, Kyoto and Xi'an. She is a Sociétaire of the Salon d'Automne.

      6. 06

        Full circle, in La Clusaz

        She also founded Espace Créa-Tea-Vité, a gallery, studio and retreat space in La Clusaz. Her parents, it turns out, were wrong.

      04Short bio · for press and applications

      Lisa Cirenza is a painter based in the French Alps. As a National Science Foundation scholar with the Juneau Icefield Research Program, she worked as a field researcher on glaciological expeditions across the Juneau Icefield, and later co-authored The Power of Ice, a children's book on glaciers and field science illustrated with her expedition photographs. She studied applied earth sciences at Stanford University and the École des Mines de Paris, and holds a master's in international policy studies from Stanford. Trained in old-masters oil technique and in sumi ink in Japan, she works with water, pigments, heat and wax. After years of painting people and the forces that divide them, from commuters on the London Underground to refugees, she now paints light, water, rock and time. Her work is held in the Stanford Hospital collection alongside works by David Hockney and Robert Rauschenberg, and in private collections in Europe, the United States and Asia. It has been praised in the Financial Times, and exhibited at the Grand Palais in Paris, in Venice during the Biennale, and in New York, Miami, London, Kyoto and Xi'an.
